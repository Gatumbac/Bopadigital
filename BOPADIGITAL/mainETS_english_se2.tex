\chapter{INTRODUCTION AND PROJECT CONTEXT}

\section{Project Context}
BOHORQUEZ \& PAUTA CORP. BOPACORP S.A. is a business-to-business (B2B) company that provides telecommunications products and services to corporate clients. Sales advisors prospect for clients, schedule visits, manage negotiations, collect documents, and follow up on service activation. Supervisors and managers need updated information about sales, field activity, documents, and commercial performance.

Before BOPADIGITAL, many activities relied on spreadsheets, shared folders, messaging applications, and email. These separate tools made it difficult to keep one current view of each client and negotiation. BOPADIGITAL was created to centralize this commercial process.

The project context and initial requirements were established during Software Engineering I. The requirements document defined the problem, stakeholders, requirements, use cases, prototypes, and initial risks. The client accepted this baseline on November 4, 2025. During Software Engineering II, the team implemented the system through Scrum sprints and prepared the final documentation and deployment evidence.

\section{Problem Statement}
The initial analysis identified three operational bottlenecks in BOPACORP's commercial process:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Document delivery and operational delay:} Advisors collect contracts, identification documents, taxpayer information, and other files. Physical or separate delivery channels can cause office trips and delay the review.
    \item \textbf{Limited visibility of field activity:} Supervisors and managers lacked one view of visits, negotiation progress, and advisor activities. Monitoring depended on messages and manual updates.
    \item \textbf{Manual sales tracking and reporting:} Portfolios, negotiations, and performance indicators were managed in spreadsheets and manual reports. This made follow-up and decision-making more difficult.
\end{enumerate}

BOPADIGITAL addresses these problems through one platform for public information, commercial management, documents, and reports.

\section{Project Description and Evolution}
BOPADIGITAL combines a public website, an internal CRM, a mobile advisor application, a REST API, and shared TypeScript contracts and validation schemas. The public website provides services, contact, content administration, and employability workflows. The internal applications manage clients, negotiations, visits, documents, supervision, reports, and role-based access.

The mobile repository, \texttt{bopacorp-mobile}, uses Expo and React Native. It contains advisor routes for authentication, clients, negotiations, documents, and settings. The testing and demonstration chapters document the verified mobile workflows.

The project started with requirements and prototypes in Software Engineering I. In Software Engineering II, the team added quality checks, frontend and API tests, risk-based testing, deployment and installation guides, user manuals, and acceptance evidence.

\section{Project Objectives}
The general objective is \textbf{to digitize and centralize} BOPACORP's B2B commercial lifecycle to improve efficiency, information access, and management visibility.

The specific objectives are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{To support} the registration and management of corporate clients and commercial prospects.
    \item \textbf{To track} negotiations, visits, statuses, and relevant commercial observations.
    \item \textbf{To enable} advisors to manage field information and documents through web and mobile workflows.
    \item \textbf{To centralize} the review and approval status of documents required by the commercial process.
    \item \textbf{To provide} supervisors and managers with consolidated information for monitoring and decisions.
    \item \textbf{To offer} a public service catalog, a content management interface, and an employability workflow for external users and candidates.
    \item \textbf{To enforce} authentication, shared data contracts, quality checks, and repeatable deployment.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Project Scope and Exclusions}
The final scope is organized around the following components:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Public Website:} company information, service catalog, contact, and employability workflows.
    \item \textbf{Content Management System:} authorized management of public content, catalog data, and media.
    \item \textbf{CRM:} role-based management of clients, negotiations, visits, documents, supervision, reports, organization, and employability.
    \item \textbf{Mobile Advisor Application:} advisor access to clients, negotiations, and documents through Expo/React Native.
    \item \textbf{REST API and Shared Contracts:} authentication, authorization, business services, validation, persistence, and TypeScript/Zod contracts.
    \item \textbf{Deployment and Documentation:} CI, deployment, installation, manuals, testing evidence, and client acceptance.
\end{itemize}

The client removed the Offer Matrices module from the final scope. Matrix construction, approval, subsidy calculations, and related calculations are excluded from the final features and scenarios. Historical references to this module are legacy material.

The final report includes only features in the agreed scope and supported by the evidence package. Other capabilities are limitations or future work.

\section{Stakeholders, Roles and Target Users}
The main stakeholders and target users are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Business Client:} views the catalog and contacts BOPACORP.
    \item \textbf{Sales Advisor:} manages assigned clients, negotiations, visits, and documents.
    \item \textbf{Supervisor or Sales Manager:} monitors advisors, progress, activities, and documents.
    \item \textbf{Documentation Coordinator:} reviews negotiation documents and updates their status.
    \item \textbf{Management or Executive Board:} consults reports and performance information.
    \item \textbf{Sales Advisor Candidate:} views vacancies and submits an application.
    \item \textbf{Web Administrator:} manages public content, catalog data, and CMS information.
    \item \textbf{Client Representative:} reviews sprint increments and signs acceptance records.
\end{itemize}

\section{Project Dimensions and Demonstration Scope}
The initial requirements baseline contains 68 user stories. Seven belong to the Offer Matrices
module and six belong to its Supervision and Approvals dependency. These 13 records are retained
for historical traceability but are excluded from the active product scope, leaving 55 active
stories. The remaining non-MAT stories stay in the historical requirements baseline. The
requirements baseline and the sprint plan represent different planning levels.

The demonstration scope comprises the following scenarios:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A visitor filters the catalog, views a service, and sends a contact request.
    \item A candidate views a vacancy, receives validation feedback, and submits a PDF resume.
    \item An administrator edits CMS or catalog data and checks the public result.
    \item A sales advisor registers a client, manages a negotiation, records a visit, and handles a document.
    \item A supervisor or coordinator reviews work and approves or rejects a document with a reason.
    \item A manager views reports, applies filters, and exports a result.
    \item An advisor uses the mobile client for clients, negotiations, and documents.
\end{enumerate}

Later chapters and appendices contain the detailed demonstration, tests, and user instructions. This chapter defines the project context and scope.

\subsection{Permission Enforcement}
The \emph{authenticate} middleware verifies the bearer token and loads the active user and roles. The \emph{authorize} middleware checks the permission required by the route. Permissions use the \emph{module.action} format, such as \emph{users.read} or \emph{catalog\_items.create}. Invalid identity returns unauthorized; missing permission returns forbidden.

Roles group permissions, while routes declare the required permission. Validation follows authorization. Services enforce ownership and state rules.

\section{Shared Contracts and Data Architecture}
The shared contract package is the integration boundary between the API and its clients. It provides common primitives, schemas, enums, and response wrappers. The package is published as \emph{@bopacorp/shared} and is consumed by the API, public website, CRM, and mobile repository.

The request flow has four steps. Routes select the endpoint and add middleware. Controllers handle HTTP data. Services apply business rules and run Drizzle queries. The database stores or returns data:

\begin{center}
\emph{routes} $\rightarrow$ \emph{controller} $\rightarrow$ \emph{service} $\rightarrow$ \emph{database}
\end{center}

The API uses one response envelope. Successful responses include data; paginated responses also include metadata; errors include \emph{success: false}, a stable code, and a message. This keeps client error handling consistent.

\section{Build Automation Configuration}
The BOPADIGITAL implementation uses a repository-oriented automation model. The API, CRM, Web,
Mobile, and Shared repositories contain the application or contract source code, while Deploy
contains the container and reverse-proxy configuration. This separation allows each repository to
validate its own responsibilities while preserving a common quality contract across the platform.

The JavaScript and TypeScript repositories use Node.js 22, npm lockfiles, and reproducible
installation through \texttt{npm ci}. Their common validation sequence is:

\begin{center}
\texttt{npm ci} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{format:check} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{lint}
$\rightarrow$ \texttt{typecheck} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{test:coverage} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{build}
\end{center}

The \texttt{check} script combines the non-mutating quality checks used by developers and
continuous integration. Formatting changes are performed explicitly through the formatting script
or through the staged-file hook; the CI check does not rewrite the workspace. This distinction makes
the result reproducible and prevents a successful pipeline from concealing uncommitted changes.

The API uses a modular monolith structure. Its build runs the TypeScript compiler and resolves the
compiled aliases before packaging the application. The CRM and Web applications use the Vite build
pipeline, while Mobile uses the Expo build pipeline. Shared compiles its domain schemas and
declaration files before publication. All application repositories execute automated tests and
produce a coverage report as part of their quality workflow.

Deploy is included as operational automation rather than application source. Its validation covers
Docker Compose, Caddy, service names, ports, required variables, and image references without
exposing credential values. It is therefore reported as operational automation, not as a TypeScript
code-quality repository.

\section{Coding Standards and Enforcers}
The project applies one coding-quality baseline across its TypeScript repositories. TypeScript uses
explicit strict checking, unused-code diagnostics, fall-through protection, and verbatim module
syntax. Biome provides formatting and linting from the same repository configuration. The common
format is two spaces, a maximum line width of 100 characters, single quotes, semicolons, and ES5
trailing commas. Unused imports and variables are errors, and explicit \texttt{any} is rejected so
that type information remains available across the API and client boundaries.

The standard also defines how source code is organized. Identifiers and source-level documentation
use English naming, while user-facing messages remain localized when required by the product. The
API groups routes, middleware, controllers, services, and database access by domain. CRM and Web
group pages, components, forms, hooks, and services by feature. Mobile keeps screens, components,
contexts, and services separated according to the Expo application structure. Shared exposes
explicit domain barrels and derives TypeScript types from Zod schemas instead of duplicating request
and response definitions.

\section{Git Hooks and Commit Validation}
The repository workflow applies quality checks before code reaches the shared branch. Husky owns the
Git hook lifecycle through the pinned \href{https://github.com/Bopacorp/bopacorp-api/blob/4018bd58c6856e9258b7e79b0b91eeaa852a5459/.husky/pre-commit}{\texttt{pre-commit}}
and \href{https://github.com/Bopacorp/bopacorp-api/blob/4018bd58c6856e9258b7e79b0b91eeaa852a5459/.husky/commit-msg}{\texttt{commit-msg}}
files. lint-staged limits local formatting and linting to staged source files, and
\href{https://github.com/Bopacorp/bopacorp-api/blob/4018bd58c6856e9258b7e79b0b91eeaa852a5459/commitlint.config.js}{\texttt{commitlint.config.js}}
validates the commit message against the Conventional Commits format. The pre-commit hook
therefore protects the smallest relevant change set, while CI repeats the full repository checks on
the complete revision.

The standardized commit flow is:

\begin{enumerate}
\item The developer stages the intended files.
\item lint-staged runs Biome on the staged TypeScript, JavaScript, and related source files.
\item The pre-commit hook stops the commit when the staged source cannot satisfy the local rules.
\item The commit-msg hook validates the commit type, optional scope, subject, and message format.
\item CI installs the lockfile-defined dependencies and repeats formatting, linting, type checking,
      tests, coverage, and build validation.
\end{enumerate}

API, CRM, Web, Mobile, and Shared use the same hook contract. Deploy uses repository-specific
operational validation instead of application-language hooks because its quality risks are Compose
configuration and proxy routing. These controls are limited to the implementation and deployment
repositories covered by this chapter.

\section{SOLID Principles and Design Patterns}
The implementation applies SOLID principles as design criteria for concrete boundaries rather than as
labels attached to entire repositories. The most visible application is the separation of the API
request pipeline. Routes declare access and validation, controllers coordinate transport concerns,
services execute domain operations, and shared error classes represent HTTP outcomes. The resulting
boundaries make changes easier to locate and reduce the risk of repeating cross-cutting behavior.

The following examples connect the principles to current source files:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Single Responsibility:} API controllers coordinate transport while services contain
      domain operations; in Web, \texttt{cms.service.ts} communicates with the API while
      \texttt{useContentBlocks.ts} manages loading and retry state.
\item \textbf{Open/Closed:} CRM pagination and unsaved-form behavior are implemented as reusable
      hooks and shared UI primitives, allowing new CRUD screens to reuse the behavior without
      copying its implementation.
\item \textbf{Liskov Substitution:} the specialized errors in
      \path{src/shared/errors/http-error.ts} extend the common HTTP error contract and are handled
      consistently by the error middleware.
\item \textbf{Interface Segregation:} Shared separates request and response schemas by domain, so a
      consumer imports only the contract required by its operation.
\item \textbf{Dependency Inversion:} application features depend on service and contract boundaries
      rather than embedding request parsing, validation, and response formatting in every screen or
      route. The final design documents the remaining concrete infrastructure dependencies explicitly.
\end{itemize}

Several design patterns reinforce these decisions. The API uses layered architecture and a
middleware pipeline for authentication, authorization, validation, and error handling. The Web and
CRM applications use reusable hooks and feature modules to isolate stateful behavior from visual
components. Mobile uses a service facade over Axios, a Context/Provider for authentication state,
and a configured client for cross-screen communication. Shared uses a schema-contract pattern in
which Zod validation and inferred TypeScript types are exported together.

\section{TypeScript Compiler Diagnostics}
Preemptive error detection begins with the TypeScript compiler. The platform uses one typed
language across the API, CRM, public Web application, Mobile application, and Shared contracts.
The compiler therefore detects incompatible values, invalid property access, missing returns, unused
symbols, and interface mismatches before the application is executed.

The standardized compiler configuration enables strict type checking together with additional
protections for indexed access, optional properties, return paths, and unused code. The Shared
package extends this protection across repository boundaries because the clients and API consume the
same typed contract definitions. The exact compiler options are documented once in
\hyperref[lst:chapter4_tsconfig]{Listing~\ref*{lst:chapter4_tsconfig}}.

Local validation uses \texttt{tsc --noEmit} so that the source is checked without producing
temporary output. The production build then performs the corresponding compilation step and creates
the artifact consumed by deployment. This separation makes type correctness visible before runtime
while preserving a reproducible build process.

\section{Biome Static Analysis}
Biome complements the compiler by examining source structure and coding conventions. The common
configuration enables formatting, import organization, and recommended lint rules across the
TypeScript repositories. The baseline uses two-space indentation, a 100-character line width,
single quotes, semicolons, and consistent trailing commas. Unused imports and variables are treated
as quality violations, while explicit \texttt{any} is rejected where the repository contract requires
complete type information. The complete Biome configuration is available in
\hyperref[lst:chapter4_biome]{Listing~\ref*{lst:chapter4_biome}}.

Biome is used through two different purposes: the formatting command applies an intentional local
correction, while \texttt{format:check} and \texttt{lint} verify the result without rewriting the
workspace. This distinction is important in continuous integration because a successful validation
must describe the submitted revision, not silently modify it.

The result is a single feedback mechanism for formatting defects, import inconsistencies, unused
code, and suspicious patterns. These diagnostics are intentionally complementary to TypeScript:
Biome evaluates source quality and structure, whereas the compiler evaluates type relationships and
program consistency.

\section{Hooks and Continuous Integration Gates}
The preventive process has a local layer and a repository layer. Their implementation is documented
once in \hyperref[tab:chapter4_commit_controls]{Table~\ref*{tab:chapter4_commit_controls}} and in
the complete workflow shown in
\hyperref[lst:chapter4_ci_pipeline]{Listing~\ref*{lst:chapter4_ci_pipeline}}. In this chapter,
the important relationship is that local hooks provide immediate feedback for staged changes, while
continuous integration repeats the contract on a clean runner before merge and publication.

The execution evidence is also presented once in Chapter~4:
\hyperref[fig:chapter4_hooks_commitlint]{Figure~\ref*{fig:chapter4_hooks_commitlint}} shows the
commit validation, and
\hyperref[fig:chapter4_quality_output_part1]{Figure~\ref*{fig:chapter4_quality_output_part1}} plus
\hyperref[fig:chapter4_quality_output_part2]{Figure~\ref*{fig:chapter4_quality_output_part2}} show
the quality results. Chapter~6 uses the resulting test artifacts to analyze test levels and risk
coverage.

\section{Observed Preventive-Quality Results}
The final evidence package records the preventive checks against revision-specific repository states.
For the API, revision \texttt{4018bd5} produced successful lint, TypeScript validation, security
audit, coverage-enabled test execution, production build, and coverage artifact upload. The same
standardized sequence is applied to CRM, Web, Mobile, and Shared, with the Shared package adding
declaration, type-level, package, and compatibility checks for its consumer boundary. The repository
revisions and evidence roles are summarized in
\hyperref[tab:chapter4_repository_automation]{Table~\ref*{tab:chapter4_repository_automation}}.

The observed result is a layered quality gate rather than a single tool result. A source change must
first satisfy the compiler and Biome, then pass the local hook, and finally pass the clean-runner
workflow. This order shortens feedback time during development and prevents a change that only
works in one local workspace from being accepted as a final artifact.

These controls do not replace functional or acceptance testing. They establish that the submitted
source is structurally valid, consistently formatted, type-safe, and buildable before

test cases, risk priorities, coverage, incidents, and retests.
